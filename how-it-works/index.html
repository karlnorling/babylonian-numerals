How numbers are drawn

Babylonian scribes wrote with a reed stylus pressed into wet clay. Numbers used just two marks: a corner wedge for ten and an upright wedge for one. Up to five corner wedges and nine upright ones make a digit from 1 to 59, with the tens on the left.

Places

Section titled “Places”

Places run left to right, most significant first, each worth sixty times the next:

Place from the right Worth
1st 1
2nd 60
3rd 3,600
4th 216,000

So 3661 is 1 × 3600 + 1 × 60 + 1, and a day of 86,400 seconds is 24, 0, 0.

Zero and empty places

Section titled “Zero and empty places”

For most of Babylonian history, scribes wrote no zero: an empty place was a gap, or nothing at all, and readers worked out the size from context. Late Babylonian texts mark empty places with two slanted wedges. This library draws that placeholder by default, and zero: 'space' leaves the gap.

Fractions

Section titled “Fractions”

Base 60 is good at fractions: a half is 30 sixtieths, a third is 20 and a quarter is 15. Every decimal fraction is exact in base 60, because 10ⁿ divides 60ⁿ.

Scribes didn’t mark where the whole number ended. This library puts a dot on the baseline there.

√2 on a clay tablet

The tablet YBC 7289 gives the square root of 2 as 1;24,51,10, which is 1.4142129…, correct to about six decimal places. formatSexagesimal writes numbers in that same notation.

Negatives

Section titled “Negatives”

Babylonian numerals had no negative numbers. This library starts a negative number with a horizontal wedge.
